In a randomized pilot trial we compared ATG (standard Arm A, 4.5 mg/kg) with ATG plus PTCy (experimental Arm BE, ATG 4.5 mg/
kg, PTCy 100 mg/kg). The primary safety endpoint was overall survival at 100 days. We analyzed 79 patients with AML (n = 55) or
MDS (n = 24). Median age (range) was 59 (19 to 74), 47 (59.5%) were male. Conditioning was either myeloablative (n = 49) or
reduced intensity (n = 30). Donors were 8/8 HLA-matched. Overall survival at 100 days was 95.0% (37 of 39 patients) vs 94.9% (38 of
40 patients) in arm A vs arm BE (p > 0.9). In arms A versus BE, serious adverse events (SAEs) occurred in 26 (65.0%) and 25 (64.1%)
patients, graft failure occurred in 3 and 1 patients (p > 0.9), median (range) days to neutrophil engraftment were 19 (10–45) versus
22 (15–56) days p = 0.007), cytomegalovirus and Epstein-Barr virus reactivations occurred in 11 and 7 patients (p = 0.45) and in 6
and 5 patients (p = 0.29). Overall survival at 12 months was 72.5% versus 75.8% (p = 0.79), cumulative incidence at 6 months of
relapse was 15.0% versus 15.7% (p = 0.88) and of non-relapse mortality (NRM) 7.5% versus 8.0 (p = 0.45). The combination of ATG
and PTCy can safely be used in phase III trials.

INTRODUCTION
Anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) and Post-Transplant Cyclopho-
sphamide (PTCy) are used in most hematopoietic cell transplants
(HCT) to prevent graft-versus-host disease (GVHD). The efﬁcacy
of both ATG [1–6] and PTCy [7, 8], each alone, in preventing GVHD
in
matched
donor
transplants,
has
been
well
established
in randomized controlled trials. However, there have been
no published randomized trials comparing ATG and PTCy
with each other. As well, neither ATG nor PTCy in isolation
completely prevents GVHD, so we became interested in combin-
ing both together (ATG/PTCy) in an attempt to further improve
results.
The above cited randomized data of ATG or PTCy alone that
inform the present study span a range of donor types, graft sources,
conditioning intensities, and dosing of ATG and ATLG. The
randomized trials of ATG in matched donor transplantation were
conducted predominantly in unrelated-donor recipients of myeloa-
blative conditioning, used peripheral blood stem cells in most cases,
and employed rabbit ATG at total doses of 4.5–15 mg/kg or ATLG
(Anti-T-lymphocyte immunoglobulin) at 30 or 60 mg/kg. Walker [3]
additionally included patients who received reduced-intensity
conditioning. For PTCy in matched donor transplantation, dosing
of cyclophosphamide was, in contrast to that in ATG trials, uniform at
100 mg total dose. Bolaños-Meade [7] demonstrated superior GVHD-

mofetil compared with tacrolimus/methotrexate in recipients of
reduced-intensity conditioning and predominantly peripheral blood
grafts from HLA-matched or 7/8 mismatched donors, and Curtis [8]

tacrolimus/methotrexate, supporting an inference that combination
with a calcineurin inhibitor is required for optimal PTCy beneﬁt.
To assess the literature on combined ATG/PTCy therapy we
performed a Medline Search of studies up till the time of
submission, using a broad strategy of “((controlled clinical trials OR
comparative trials OR phase III trials) AND (antithymocyte globulin
OR antilymphocyte globulin) AND (post-transplant cyclopho-
sphamide OR PTCy)). This strategy yielded forty-three studies,
and these were supplemented by ﬁve studies from personal ﬁles.
From these we selected seven publications that were either
reviews (2), randomized controlled trials in which ATG/PTCy was
compared to a control group (2), or non-randomized comparative
trials of matched donor transplants only in which ATG/PTCy was
compared with a control group, whether center (2) or registry
based (1). The commonest reasons for excluding studies were the
use
of
haploidentical
donors
for
non-randomized
studies,
comparisons of ATG with PTCy each alone, and uncontrolled
series in which ATG or PTCy were used alone. The seven selected
studies are described below.
Two randomized controlled trials of combined ATG/PTCy
involved matched and haplo-identical donors respectively. Zu
[10] compared a combination of “low dose” ATG (6 mg) and PTCy
(40 mg) with ATG 10 mg alone in matched unrelated donor
transplants. There were statistically signiﬁcant reductions in the
cumulative incidences of grade II–IV acute GVHD (AGVHD), and of
chronic GVHD (CGVHD), and improved graft-versus-host disease
free- and disease-free survival (GRFS). The second randomized trial
was in haploidentical transplants using a low total dose of PTCy
29 mg/kg and bone marrow grafts, that was halted at interim
analysis for futility against the hypothesized improvement over
the haploidentical standard [11].
One controlled registry study [12] of combined ATG/PTCy in
matched donor transplants was identiﬁed; 421 subjects receiving
PTCy alone were compared with 151 subjects receiving PTCy with
ATG. Doses of ATG were not described, CGVHD was not assessed
by NIH criteria and one-third of subjects were 9/10 mismatched.
There were no differences in the endpoints of AGVHD, CGVHD,
relapse rate or survival between the groups.
Non-randomized matched comparisons were published from
Princess Margaret Cancer Centre (PMCC), Toronto, where com-
bined ATG/PTCy had been adopted to replace ATG alone as
standard prophylaxis for all transplants starting in 2015. Two
studies describe comparisons of outcomes following combined
ATG/PTCy (after 2015) with outcomes following ATG alone (before
2015) in matched unrelated donors. Deotare [13] compared their
experience of 28 patients with fully matched donors receiving
ATG/PTCy during the years 2015 and 2016 with a matched group
of 27 patients having received ATG during years 2011 and 2013.
There was a statistically signiﬁcant decrease in cumulative
incidence of severe AGVHD following ATG/PTCy compared with
those receiving ATG and a notable decrease in CGVHD. Salas et al.
[14], also at PMCC, published on an expanded population of
similar patients receiving fully matched donor grafts, 84 patients
having received ATG 2011 to 2015 and 360 patients having
received ATG/PTCy from 2015 onward, of whom 233 patients
received a reduced dose of the ATG component from 4.5 to 2 mg
from 2018 onward. Like the earlier study by Deotare et al, ATG/
PTCy resulted in statistically signiﬁcant reductions in severe grades
of both AGVHD and CGVHD with no adverse effect on disease
relapse, non-relapse mortality (NRM) or overall survival.
Two recent reviews summarize current literature on combined ATG/
PTCy therapy. Barriga [15] concluded: “Collectively, these studies
support the potential beneﬁt of combining PTCy and ATG in reducing
GvHD without compromising survival. However, they are limited by
their retrospective nature, heterogeneity in ATG dosing and adminis-
tration schedules, and differences in transplantation era and follow-up
duration”. Chen et al. [16] point to “a lack of prospective trials” and that
“further RCTs are warranted to optimize the dosing and scheduling of
combination regimens across various transplant settings”.
METHODS
We performed a randomized, controlled, multi-centre, parallel group,
open-label, pilot trial comparing standard ATG alone with ATG plus PTCy,
to assess feasibility and safety, randomizing 85 available patients.
Eligibility and conduct
Recipients were aged ≥16 years having either acute myeloid leukemia
(AML) in remission, or myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), and fulﬁlling
centre criteria for transplant eligibility. Permitted preparative regimens
could be myeloablative or reduced intensity. Reduced intensity regimens
were ﬂudarabine combined with either busulfan or melphalan; myeloa-
blative regimes were busulfan combined with either ﬂudarabine or
cyclophosphamide. Donors were either related or unrelated and were fully
HLA-matched (8/8) at HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, or HLA-DRB1 loci using high
resolution typing. Grafts were from peripheral blood. Supportive therapies
were according to local institutional practices. We completed the study in
six transplantation centers in Canada and two in Australia. Trial manage-
ment was shared by Ozmosis Research Inc, Toronto, ON, Canada, and the
Australasian Leukemia Lymphoma Group, Richmond, VIC, Australia.
Randomization and masking
We used minimization [17] to assign patients to either arm A (ATG) or BE
(ATG/PTCy). Minimization factors were a priori risk factors for cGVHD,
relapse and mortality, these being participant age (16–30, 31–50, >50);
female donor for male recipient; choice of donor (related vs. unrelated);
donor age (< =30, 31–50, >50); disease type (AML or MDS); disease risk
index (low, intermediate, high or very high); type of preparative regimen
(myeloablative or reduced intensity), and co-morbidity by augmented HCT-
CI [18] (0–3 or >3); transplant centre was included to balance for
differences in support practices. A list of transplant centers and details of
the assignment procedure are available in the Supplemental ﬁle.
Transplant Procedures
Patients in the standard ATG group (A) received rabbit anti-thymocyte globulin
(Thymoglobuline, Sanoﬁ–Genzyme, Lyon, France) intravenously at a total dose
of 4.5 mg/kg over 3 days (0.5 mg/kg on day −2, and 2.0 mg/kg on days −1 and
+1). The dose was calculated on actual body weight, and no reductions were
speciﬁed. Dose interruptions for reactions were permitted but it was expected
that the entire daily dose would be infused on the same day as initiated.
Patients in the experimental group (BE) received ATG as for group A and also
received PTCy 50 mg/kg on post-transplant days 3 and 4. All patients received
a calcineurin inhibitor. Methotrexate 15 mg/m2 on day +1 and 10 mg/m2 on
days +3, +6, and +11 was added for patients receiving myeloablative
conditioning and randomized to group A, but not to those randomized to
group BE, who received a CNI alone. Mycophenolate (3 centres) or
methotrexate (3 centres) was added to those receiving reduced intensity
conditioning and randomized to group A but not to those randomized to
group BE who received a CNI alone. Study management and supportive care
restrictions are detailed in the Supplemental ﬁle. Acute and chronic graft
versus host disease were diagnosed and graded by standard criteria [19, 20].
Sample Size
As this was a pilot trial, a formal sample size calculation was not performed,
consistent with CONSORT extension guidance for pilot and feasibility trials
[21]. A target accrual of 80 patients was selected as the maximum available,
and sufﬁcient to inform the safety of the experimental combination.

survival in experimental arm BE would be at least 90% that of the standard

Statistical analysis
The protocol speciﬁed that the primary analysis would be conducted
according to the intention-to-treat principle. Of 85 randomized patients,
6 were excluded from the analysis population—5 who were retro-
spectively found not to have met one or more eligibility criteria
at the time of randomization, and 1 whose planned transplantation
was cancelled—yielding the modiﬁed intention-to-treat population of 79
patients on which the results below are based. Decisions regarding these
exclusions were taken prospectively by the trial group in consultation
with the study statistician. All remaining participants were analyzed
according to their randomized treatment assignment. A complete list of
analytic methods is included in the Supplemental File.

for at least 12 months, at which time median follow up of living
patients was 24.1 months (range 11.89–26.71) and 23.7 months
(range 11.99–25.07) for arms A and BE respectively. Our intention was
to transplant 80 eligible patients. Between October 26th, 2020, and
February 7th, 2024, we randomly assigned 85 patients (Fig. 1), six of
whom were excluded leaving 79 patients available for analysis. Of the
six patients, ﬁve were found to be ineligible and one had his/her
transplantation cancelled. Forty patients were analyzed from arm A
(ATG alone) and 39 patients from experimental arm BE (ATG/PTCy).
Baseline characteristics did not differ between the treatment groups
(Table 1). Median age was 59 years, range 19–74 with males 59.5%.

Outcomes at long-term follow-up
After a median follow up of 23.9 months (range 11.89–26.71), 53
patients remained alive and their data were analyzed. The major
endpoints are summarized and illustrated in Table 4 and Fig. 2a–d.
Overall survival (Fig. 2a) at 12-months was 72.5% (% SE 7.1) in arm
A and 75.8% (SE 7.0) in arm BE (log rank p = 0.79). Cumulative
incidence of NRM at 6-months (Fig. 2b) was 7.5% (SE 4.2) in arm A
and 8.0% (SE 4.) in arm BE (Gray’s test p = 0.45). Relapses were
seen in 8 patients in each arm. Cumulative incidence of relapse at
6-months (Fig. 2c) was 15.0% (SE 5.7) in arm A and 15.7% (SE 6.0)
in arm BE (Gray’s test p = 0.88). Relapse-free survival at 12-months
was 60.0% (SE 7.8) in arm A and 65.9% (SE 7.7) in arm BE (log rank
p = 0.66). Moderate or severe chronic GVHD- and relapse-free
survival (CRFS; Fig. 2d) at 12-months was 56.1% (SE 7.8) in arm A
and 60.6% (SE 7.9) in arm BE (log rank p = 0.69). GRFS (Fig. 2e) at
12 months was 52.5% (SE 7.9) in arm A and 66.67% (SE 7.7) in arm
BE (log-rank p = 0.27). Acute GVHD (all grades) was seen in 18
patients in arm A and 9 patients in arm BE, and CI at 6-months was
42.5% (SE 8.0) in arm A and 23.7% (SE 7.0) in arm BE (Gray’s test
p = 0.04). Acute GVHD Grades II–IV were seen in 11 in arm A and 5
patients in arm BE, and CI at 6-months was 25.0% (SE7.0) in arm A
and 13.1% (SE 5.6) in arm BE, (Gray’s test p = 0.13), and grade III/IV
in 5 patients in arm A and 1 patient in arm BE, CI at 6-months 10.0
(SE 4.8) and 2.7% (SE 2.7), Gray’s test p = 0.10) in arms A and BE
respectively. Late AGVHD (after day 100) occurred in 3 patients in
each arm. CGVHD has been reported to date in 6 patients and 3
patients in arms A and BE respectively with moderate or severe
grades in 2 patients in each arm. Steroid and/or immunosuppres-
sive agents were being administered at 12 months for 6 patients
and 2 patients in arms A and BE respectively.
DISCUSSION
This randomized pilot trial, intended to assess the safety of ATG/
PTCy when compared with standard ATG, achieved its primary
endpoint of survival in the ATG/PTCy arm being at least 90% of
that in the ATG arm, at 100 days. A variety of secondary safety
endpoints showed no excess toxicity in the experimental arm
(ATG/PTCy), nor, after minimal follow up of 24 months, adverse
effects on survival, rate of infections or rate of disease relapse.
Our ﬁndings are best interpreted alongside the contemporary
randomized data on each component of the experimental
combination. The rates of grade II–IV acute GVHD observed in
the standard ATG arm (15.0% at day 100; cumulative incidence
25.0% at 6 months) are consistent with those reported in our
group’s previous matched-donor ATG trial [3] and with other
randomized
ATG
trials
in
matched
donor
transplantation
[1, 2, 4–6], supporting the internal validity of the standard arm.
The further reduction in acute GVHD seen with the addition of
PTCy in the experimental arm (cumulative incidence 13.1% at
6 months for grade II–IV) is directionally consistent with the
magnitude of acute GVHD reduction reported in randomized trials

The two randomized trials were described in the introduction.
The trial by Zu [22], in matched donors, while showing a reduction
in GVHD used a pragmatic rather than explanatory design, with
atypical doses of ATG and PTCy and with doses of ATG differing in
each arm, limiting both the conclusions to be drawn and future
adoption into clinical practice or trial design. This contrasts with
the present study which used validated doses and a conventional
design. With respect to the study by Xu [11], the present pilot trial
differs in evaluating matched donor recipients rather than haplo-
identical donors, peripheral blood grafts rather than bone marrow
grafts, and more typical doses of ATG and PTCy.
There are several weaknesses to this study. One is deﬁning
safety under a limited primary endpoint of short-term survival.
Survival may be an insensitive measure of safety given that there
are a wide variety of potential toxicities that would also be
unacceptable for further use of the experimental treatment (ATG/
PTCy). We considered, however, that a primary endpoint based on
a variety of toxicities would be difﬁcult to deﬁne, and debatable,
while a decrease of survival would provide an undebatable end to
further investigation of the experimental treatment. Nevertheless,
the concept of safety in this trial is well supported by a variety of
secondary endpoints describing the well-known complications of
bone marrow transplantation. The choice of 100 days relates to
the span of time during which most transplant-related toxicities
occur but the results at long term follow up also point to safety. A
second weakness of this study is built into the objective, which is
limited to safety, and therefore there is no substantial information
on efﬁcacy. A further weakness is the lack of current knowledge of
the optimal doses of both ATG and PTCy. While the doses of both
ATG and PTCy used in the current study have been validated
against controls, they may not be correct for optimal outcomes of
efﬁcacy and toxicities.
We consider, based on results in this trial, in particular those
pointing to safety and the trend in the incidence of acute GVHD,
together with the already widespread use of the combination
[12, 23, 24], that PTCy/ATG is sufﬁciently safe to be further
investigated for the prevention of GVHD in a large randomized
trial sufﬁciently powered to assess incidences of acute and
chronic GVHD.
