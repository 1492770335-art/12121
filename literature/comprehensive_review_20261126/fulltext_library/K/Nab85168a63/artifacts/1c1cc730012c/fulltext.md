Efficacy and Safety of CLAG-Based Sequential 
Conditioning Followed by Allogeneic Hematopoietic 
Stem Cell Transplantation in Non-Remission 
Relapsed or Refractory Acute Leukemia
Shiyu Chen, Xiaohan Zhang, Xiaoqing Li, Xin Du
Department of Hematology, Clinical College of Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital, Anhui Medical University, Shenzhen, Guangdong, People’s 
Republic of China
Correspondence: Xin Du, Email 2336020025@stu.ahmu.edu.cn
Background: The optimal bridge-to-transplant strategy for patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) acute leukemia who remain in 
non-remission (NR) is undefined. We evaluated the strategy-level use of CLAG (cladribine, cytarabine, and granulocyte colony- 
stimulating factor) as part of sequential conditioning, rather than as stand-alone salvage therapy, before allogeneic hematopoietic stem 
cell transplantation (allo-HSCT).
Methods: This single-center retrospective case series included nine consecutive patients with R/R acute leukemia and bone marrow 
blasts ≥10% who underwent CLAG-based sequential conditioning followed by allo-HSCT between January 2021 and July 2023. 
Engraftment, transplant-related complications, non-relapse mortality, relapse, disease-free survival (DFS), and overall survival (OS) 
were evaluated descriptively; no control group was available.
Results: All nine patients achieved neutrophil and platelet engraftment and complete donor chimerism at approximately day +30. At 
a routine MFC/NGF analytical sensitivity of 10−4, all patients had negative bone marrow assessments at day +30 and month +3; 
abnormal/leukemic cells subsequently re-emerged in all five patients who relapsed, at months 4.1–9.8. Seven of nine patients (77.8%) 
developed infections during neutropenia, including one severe pulmonary infection with probable Aspergillus that improved with 
antifungal therapy. GVHD occurred in 3/9 patients (33.3%), and hemorrhagic cystitis occurred in 2/9 (22.2%). No non-relapse 
mortality occurred. Five patients (55.6%) relapsed, and all five deaths were relapse-related. Estimated 2-year DFS and OS were both 
44.4% (95% confidence interval, 13.6–71.9%).
Conclusion: In this highly selected cohort of nine NR patients, CLAG-based sequential conditioning permitted engraftment without non- 
relapse mortality, but infection and relapse remained frequent. These findings support technical feasibility only and should be regarded as 
exploratory and hypothesis-generating; efficacy or superiority cannot be established without larger prospective, multicenter, controlled studies.
Keywords: allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, acute leukemia, relapsed, refractory, non-remission, CLAG
Introduction
Relapsed or refractory acute leukemia (R/R AL) remains a major therapeutic challenge and is associated with limited 
survival. Conventional intensive salvage regimens, including FLAG (fludarabine, cytarabine, and granulocyte colony- 
stimulating factor [G-CSF]), produce incomplete and often short-lived responses in many patients.1 Mutation-directed 
agents and venetoclax-based combinations have expanded treatment options in selected patients with acute myeloid 
leukemia (AML),2 whereas blinatumomab and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy have improved response and 
bridging opportunities in relapsed/refractory acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL).3 Nevertheless, target availability, prior 
resistance, treatment-related toxicity, and rapid disease progression continue to limit the applicability of these 
approaches.

For patients who remain in non-remission (NR), allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) remains 
a potentially curative treatment option.4–8 Higher pre-transplant disease burden is associated with increased relapse and 
inferior survival after allo-HSCT,9–11 and failure to achieve complete remission is generally associated with poorer survival in 
AML.12 A clinically relevant knowledge gap is therefore how to achieve additional cytoreduction immediately before allo- 
HSCT without causing prohibitive toxicity or preventing timely transplantation. Existing approaches include intensive salvage 
or CLAG-based bridging,1,13,14 venetoclax- or mutation-directed treatment in selected patients,2 and intensified conditioning 
strategies.15,16 However, evidence specifically derived from NR populations remains limited and heterogeneous.
CLAG combines cladribine, cytarabine, and G-CSF. Cladribine has demonstrated antileukemic activity in AML.17,18 
Mechanistically, cladribine may enhance the intracellular activity of cytarabine, whereas G-CSF priming is intended to recruit 
quiescent leukemic cells into the cell cycle and increase chemosensitivity.18,19 CLAG has also demonstrated clinical activity as 
salvage therapy in relapsed/refractory AML.2,20 A recent propensity-score-matched study evaluated CLAG as a separate 
bridging therapy before allo-HSCT in relapsed/refractory AML.14 The present study examines a related but distinct institutional 
approach in which CLAG was incorporated into an uninterrupted sequential conditioning platform immediately followed by allo- 
HSCT. This represents a strategy-level application rather than a new drug combination or mechanistic innovation.
We hypothesized that CLAG-based sequential conditioning could be delivered to selected NR patients without compro­
mising engraftment or causing excessive non-relapse mortality. We therefore assessed engraftment, transplant-related 
complications, relapse, DFS, and OS in a consecutive single-center case series. Given the cohort size and absence of 
a comparator, the study was designed to evaluate feasibility and generate hypotheses rather than establish treatment efficacy.
Materials and Methods
Study Design and Patient Selection
This retrospective, single-center case series included nine consecutive patients with R/R acute leukemia who remained in 
NR immediately before conditioning and underwent CLAG-based sequential conditioning followed by allo-HSCT at The 
First Affiliated Hospital of Shenzhen University/Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital between January 2021 and 
July 2023. NR was operationally defined as bone marrow blasts ≥10% before conditioning. The screening frame 
comprised 323 consecutive patients with acute leukemia who underwent evaluation for allo-HSCT at our center from 
January 2014 to July 2023. As shown in Figure 1, 304 patients were excluded because they achieved remission or 
cytoreduction to <10% marrow blasts, four because of severe organ dysfunction, and six because conditioning/trans­
plantation was not completed; nine patients met the study criteria.
The nine included patients were not selected according to post-transplant outcome. Nevertheless, the need to be 
clinically eligible for myeloablative allo-HSCT and to complete the institutional protocol introduces unavoidable 
selection bias. No contemporaneous control group treated with another bridge or conditioning strategy was available; 
consequently, all outcome analyses were descriptive and no causal attribution to CLAG was attempted.
The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Shenzhen University/Shenzhen 
Second People’s Hospital (approval No. 2024-433-01PJ). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants 
before treatment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
Eligibility Criteria
1. Diagnosis of acute leukemia according to established diagnostic criteria.
2. R/R disease with NR status immediately before conditioning, defined as bone marrow blasts ≥10%.
3. Underwent CLAG-based sequential myeloablative conditioning followed by allo-HSCT at our center.
Exclusion criteria were severe organ dysfunction, inability to tolerate or complete conditioning/transplantation because of 
intolerance or patient/family request, or achievement of remission/substantial cytoreduction before transplantation (bone 
marrow blasts <10%).

Conditioning Regimens
All patients received myeloablative conditioning. Regimen allocation was based on donor HLA matching under the 
institutional protocol: patients receiving 5–9/10 matched related grafts received CLAG plus FBCA, whereas the patient 
receiving a 10/10 matched related graft received CLAG plus BuCy. The two patients with early T-cell precursor acute 
lymphoblastic leukemia (ETP-ALL) also received decitabine.
CLAG + FBCA (5–9/10 Matched Related Donor)
1. CLAG: cladribine 10 mg/day on days −15 to −11; cytarabine 1 g/m2 every 12 h on days −15 to −11; and G-CSF 
300 microg/day on days −16 to −11.
2. FBCA: fludarabine 30 mg/m2/day on days −11 to −7; busulfan 0.8 mg/kg every 6 h on days −10 to −7; 
cyclophosphamide 60 mg/kg/day on days −6 to −5; and rabbit anti-human thymocyte immunoglobulin 2.5 mg/ 
kg/day on days −4 to −2.
3. For the two ETP-ALL patients, decitabine 25 mg/m2/day was added on days −15 to −11.
CLAG + BuCy (10/10 Matched Related Donor)
1. CLAG: cladribine 10 mg/day on days −14 to −10; cytarabine 1 g/m2 every 12 h on days −14 to −10; and G-CSF 
300 microg/day on days −15 to −10.
2. BuCy: busulfan 0.8 mg/kg every 6 h on days −9 to −6; cyclophosphamide 60 mg/kg/day on days −5 to −4; and 
rabbit anti-human thymocyte immunoglobulin 2.5 mg/kg/day on days −3 to −2.
Prophylaxis and Post-Transplant Monitoring
GVHD prophylaxis consisted of cyclosporine A (CsA), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and short-course methotrexate 
(MTX). CsA was started on day −1 at 2.5 mg/kg by continuous infusion in adults, with trough concentrations maintained 
at 100–250 microg/mL. MTX was administered at 15 mg/m2 on day +1 and 10 mg/m2 on days +3 and +6. MMF was 
started on day −11 at 30 mg/kg/day orally and continued until day +50.
Institutional infection prophylaxis included antibacterial, antiviral, and antifungal agents. Posaconazole was continued 
after transplantation for antifungal prophylaxis. Hemorrhagic cystitis prophylaxis included urinary catheterization before 
cyclophosphamide, mesna at 0, 4, and 8 h after cyclophosphamide, urine alkalinization, hydration (2000 mL/m2/day), 
and furosemide-supported diuresis.
Bone marrow disease assessment by multiparameter/next-generation flow cytometry (MFC/NGF), short tandem 
repeat (STR) chimerism, and patient-specific genetic monitoring were scheduled at baseline, day +30, and months 3, 
6, 9, 12, 18, and 24. MFC/NGF had a routine analytical sensitivity of 10−4; a reported value of 0 was classified as 
negative. Patient-level serial results are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Contemporary AML guidance emphasizes 
standardized, disease- and marker-specific interpretation of flow-cytometric and molecular MRD.21 Because this cohort 
included both AML and ETP-ALL, the sample size was small, and recurrent values frequently represented overt disease 
rather than low-level MRD, no formal MRD-outcome association was tested. Molecular or MFC/NGF recurrence could 
prompt individualized interventions such as donor lymphocyte infusion (DLI), interferon, or earlier tapering of immu­
nosuppression. The two FLT3-ITD-positive patients (cases 8 and 9) received post-transplant maintenance with gilteritinib 
beginning at approximately month +3 after allo-HSCT. Gilteritinib was started at a reduced dose of 80 mg once daily and 
was subsequently adjusted on an individual basis according to treatment tolerability and serial MFC/NGF findings. 
Because post-transplant interventions were individualized and were not administered within a standardized prospective 
protocol, their independent effects on relapse or survival could not be determined.

Outcomes and Definitions
Neutrophil engraftment was defined as the first of three consecutive days with an absolute neutrophil count ≥0.5 × 109/L. Platelet 
engraftment was defined as the first of seven consecutive days with a platelet count ≥20 × 109/L without platelet transfusion. 
Complete donor chimerism was assessed by STR analysis at approximately day +30. Non-relapse mortality was defined as death 
without preceding leukemia relapse or progression. Relapse/progression was determined from bone marrow morphology, 
molecular/cytogenetic evidence, or extramedullary disease as documented by the treating team. Acute and chronic GVHD 
diagnoses, involved organs, and severity grades were retrospectively abstracted as documented by the treating physicians in the 
source medical records. Because the original grading framework could not be uniformly verified, no retrospective reclassification 
was attempted. OS was calculated from allo-HSCT to death from any cause or last follow-up. DFS was calculated from allo- 
HSCT to documented relapse/progression or death; patients alive without morphologic disease were censored at last follow-up.
Statistical Analysis
Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10. Categorical outcomes are presented as exact counts with 
percentages, and continuous variables as medians and ranges. OS and DFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier 
method with 95% confidence intervals. Given the sample size (n = 9), disease heterogeneity, and absence of 
a comparator, no subgroup hypothesis testing, multivariable modeling, or treatment-effect estimation was performed.
Results
Cohort and Baseline Characteristics
Among 323 screened patients with acute leukemia, nine met the prespecified study criteria (Figure 1). The cohort 
included two patients with ETP-ALL and seven with AML; five were male and four were female. Pre-transplant bone 
marrow blasts ranged from 13% to 94% (median, 69%). All patients had received prior antileukemic therapy without 
achieving sustained CR/CRi before transplantation. One patient had a complex karyotype, and two (cases 8 and 9) were 
FLT3-ITD positive. Patient-level molecular, cytogenetic, and treatment characteristics are summarized in Table 2.

Conditioning Safety and Engraftment
No non-relapse deaths occurred. One patient developed drug-induced liver injury beginning on day −4, with elevated 
alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase; liver enzymes normalized by day +12 after supportive therapy. 
No other clinically significant cardiac, hepatic, or renal toxicities were recorded (Table 3).
All nine patients achieved neutrophil and platelet engraftment. Neutrophil engraftment occurred on days 14–22 
(median, day 15), and platelet engraftment on days 15–35 (median, day 23). STR analysis at approximately day +30 
showed complete donor chimerism in all patients.
Infections and Other Transplant-Related Complications
Seven of nine patients (77.8%) developed an infection during the neutropenic period. The median time to the first 
infection was day +3 (range, day −10 to day +17), with most events occurring between day −3 and day +7. One patient 
developed a severe pulmonary infection; bronchoalveolar lavage findings were consistent with probable Aspergillus 
infection, and the patient improved with voriconazole plus caspofungin. No infection-related death occurred before 
relapse. Intensive care unit utilization was not consistently available in the retrospective dataset and is therefore not 
reported.
GVHD occurred in 3/9 patients (33.3%): one patient developed skin/liver acute GVHD on day +62, one developed severe 
intestinal chronic GVHD at month 4, and one developed skin chronic GVHD at month 5. All improved after individualized 
treatment. Hemorrhagic cystitis occurred in 2/9 patients (22.2%) and improved with supportive measures (Table 4).

Relapse, Post-Transplant Interventions, and Survival
Five patients (55.6%) relapsed at a median of 5.7 months (range, 4.1–9.8); four relapses occurred between months 3 and 
6 and one between months 6 and 12. Both ETP-ALL patients (2/2) and three of seven AML patients relapsed. All five 
patients who relapsed subsequently died, generally in the context of severe infection during post-relapse therapy or, for 
case 9, after a second transplantation. At last follow-up, four patients (all with AML) were alive and disease-free.
Serial MFC/NGF assessments showed negative bone marrow results in all nine patients at day +30 and month +3. 
Abnormal/leukemic cells subsequently re-emerged in each of the five patients who relapsed: 13% in case 1 at month 
+4.1, 31% in case 7 at month +4.9, 22% and 58% in cases 4 and 9, respectively, at month +6, and 42% in case 2 at month 
+9.8. Where repeat assessments were available, the abnormal-cell percentage increased further (case 4, 87% at month +9; 
case 9, 91% at month +9; case 2, 67% at month +12). Cases 3, 5, 6, and 8 remained MFC/NGF-negative through their 
last available assessments. These observations are descriptive only and are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.
Cases 8 and 9 received gilteritinib maintenance beginning at month +3 after allo-HSCT. Case 8 remained alive and 
disease-free at 30.6 months, whereas case 9 experienced relapse at 5.8 months and died at 9.3 months. Given the two- 
patient sample and the absence of a standardized comparator, no causal inference regarding the efficacy of gilteritinib 
maintenance can be made.
The median follow-up for the entire cohort was 13.3 months (range, 4.9–39.6). Kaplan–Meier estimated 2-year DFS 
and OS were both 44.4% (95% confidence interval, 13.6–71.9%; Figure 2). Exact patient-level outcomes are presented in

Discussion
This case series evaluates a strategy-level application of CLAG as part of sequential conditioning immediately before 
allo-HSCT in patients with R/R acute leukemia who remained in NR. The principal feasibility signals were universal 
engraftment, complete donor chimerism at approximately day +30, and no non-relapse mortality. These findings should 
not be interpreted as evidence of efficacy or superiority because only nine highly selected patients were treated, no 
control group was available, and the conditioning and post-transplant interventions were heterogeneous.
Historical studies of allo-HSCT in refractory or NR acute leukemia have reported approximately 14–40% 3-year OS, 
with relapse as the dominant cause of failure.6–8 Our estimated 2-year OS of 44.4% falls within the broad range of these 
heterogeneous reports, but the 95% confidence interval was wide (13.6–71.9%), and cross-study comparison is not 
a substitute for a concurrent control. Reports of cladribine-containing sequential or conditioning approaches suggest that 
such regimens can be delivered before allo-HSCT.22–25 In addition, a larger propensity-score-matched study specifically 
evaluated CLAG as bridging therapy before allo-HSCT in relapsed/refractory AML.14 Differences in disease status, 
response before transplantation, treatment sequence, donor type, conditioning platform, and maintenance strategy 
preclude direct comparison with the present cohort.
The biological rationale for CLAG is consistent with its use as an immediate cytoreductive component: cladribine 
may enhance cytarabine activity, cytarabine inhibits leukemic proliferation, and G-CSF priming may increase cell-cycle 
entry and chemosensitivity.18,19 The contribution of the present work is therefore a patient-level description of CLAG 
incorporated into an uninterrupted sequential conditioning platform for selected NR patients, rather than a new drug 
combination or evidence that this strategy is superior to other bridging approaches. The current data cannot separate the 
contribution of CLAG from FBCA/BuCy, graft-versus-leukemia effects, donor characteristics, or post-transplant therapy.
Toxicity requires cautious interpretation. Although there was no non-relapse mortality and only one transient episode 
of drug-induced liver injury, infection occurred in 7/9 patients (77.8%), including one severe probable Aspergillus 
pneumonia. We therefore revised the description from broadly “acceptable toxicity” to a more specific statement: 
engraftment was preserved and no non-relapse death occurred, but the infectious burden was substantial. Similarly, 
relapse in 5/9 patients (55.6%) indicates that disease control remained limited despite successful transplantation.
Post-transplant relapse prevention is likely important in this population. FLT3-inhibitor maintenance, immunosup­
pression tapering, DLI, interferon, and other immune- or target-directed approaches may be considered according to 
molecular risk and clinical status. In a randomized study of post-transplant gilteritinib for FLT3-ITD-positive AML, 
relapse-free survival was not significantly improved in the overall population, although the findings suggested greater 
benefit in patients with detectable peri-transplant FLT3-ITD MRD.26 This evidence provides clinical context but does not 
establish benefit in our two treated patients, who received individualized lower-dose maintenance and had divergent 
outcomes. Serial MFC/NGF findings in this cohort were clinically coherent: all nine patients were negative at day +30 
and month +3, abnormal/leukemic cells re-emerged at or near relapse in all five patients who relapsed, and the four 
patients alive and disease-free remained negative through their last available assessments. However, this pattern should 
not be interpreted as evidence of predictive validity or treatment effect because the cohort was very small, no comparator 
was available, and recurrent values commonly represented overt disease rather than low-level MRD. Prospective 
protocols should standardize post-transplant interventions and incorporate harmonized flow-cytometric and molecular 
MRD assessment in accordance with disease-specific guidance.21
This study has several major limitations: retrospective single-center design; very small sample size; absence of 
a control group; selection of patients sufficiently fit to complete myeloablative transplantation; inclusion of both AML 
and ETP-ALL; heterogeneity in donor HLA matching and conditioning (FBCA, BuCy, and decitabine); individualized 
supportive and post-transplant treatment; lack of harmonized disease-specific molecular MRD testing; inability to 
formally evaluate the prognostic value of serial MFC/NGF kinetics; and limited statistical power. These limitations 
preclude treatment-effect estimation and restrict generalizability. The results should be viewed as a feasibility signal that 
justifies, but does not replace, prospective multicenter evaluation.

Conclusion
CLAG-based sequential conditioning followed by allo-HSCT was technically feasible in this highly selected nine-patient 
NR cohort, with universal engraftment and no non-relapse mortality. However, infections were frequent and more than 
half of the patients relapsed. The study is exploratory and hypothesis-generating and does not support routine clinical 
adoption or superiority over other bridge/conditioning strategies. Larger prospective, multicenter, controlled studies with 
standardized MRD assessment and post-transplant relapse-prevention strategies are required.
