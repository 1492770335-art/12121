Abstract
Dynamic treatment regimes (DTRs) are statistical methods that use patient-specific information to estimate an 
optimal sequence of treatments, with the end goal of maximizing a desired outcome of interest. DTRs have many 
useful applications to chronic disease management when patient characteristics are monitored over multiple 
periods of follow up to determine whether to continue a course of treatment or adopt an alternate strategy. 
Applications of DTRs to interference networks, or settings where an individual’s outcome can be affected by the 
treatments received by other individuals in the network, have received relatively little attention in the literature. 
Previous work has established DTR estimation for couples in households and in networks where general forms 
of interference are taking place, but the extent to which interference needs to be accounted for to avoid biased 
estimates of optimal treatment rules remains unknown. In this paper, we demonstrate how one such DTR 
method, dynamic weighted ordinary least squares regression (dWOLS), can be modified to account for both 
within- and between-group interference in nested hierarchical networks, and we compare our implementation 
with other dWOLS approaches with mis-specified exposure mappings. Our findings show that both the overall 
rate of treatment and the proportion of individuals experiencing interference from assigned neighbours can affect 
the performance of dWOLS regardless of the specified exposure mapping. Although standard dWOLS may be 
acceptable in some settings when the overall rate of treatment and proportion of egos assigned alters is expected 
to be low, our results demonstrate the improved performance of a dWOLS implementation that accounts for both 
within- and between-group interference, regardless of the overall rate of treatment or proportion of egos assigned 
alters.
Keywords  Dynamic treatment regime, Interference, Causal inference, Observational data

​Background
Dynamic treatment regimes (DTRs) are key statistical 
methods for developing personalized treatment strate­
gies, particularly in the context of chronic diseases where 
patient characteristics are monitored over time and a 
course of treatment may be changed in response to the 
patient’s needs. Estimating the optimal DTR over one 
or more time intervals, or stages, of treatment trans­
lates to determining the sequence of decision rules that 
maximizes a patient’s expected outcome, conditional on 
their demographic and clinical characteristics as well as 
their history of prior treatments at each stage [1]. While 
DTRs can be estimated from a special class of random­
ized trials known as sequential multiple assignment ran­
domized trials (SMARTs), they can also be constructed 
using longitudinal observational data under several key 
assumptions and by accounting for complexities such as 
time-varying confounding variables [2].
A key assumption in the DTR literature is the stable 
unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA), which states 
that an individual’s outcome is independent of the treat­
ments received by other individuals [3]. Much DTR 
development has modelled individual outcomes only in 
terms of one’s own treatments and characteristics, yet 
there are many settings known as interference networks 
where the treatments or actions taken by an individual 
can affect the outcome of others. A well-known example 
is when an individual’s susceptibility to viral infection is 
influenced by the number of household members who 
are vaccinated [4, 5], and evaluating indirect effects of 
treatments or policies is also of interest across cluster-
level trials for infectious disease research, such as the 
SEARCH study evaluating community-based strate­
gies for HIV prevention and treatment and the SHINE 
study testing strategies to reduce Staphylococcus aureus 
infection within households [6]. Figure 1 illustrates a 
directed acyclic graph (DAG) for the simplest case where 
an individual (or an ego as defined in [7]) has their out­
come affected by both their own treatment and covari­
ate as well as those of another individual (defined as an 
alter [7]). An interference network often represents the 
connections or “links” between individuals as edges of 
a pre-specified graph [8–12], and an exposure mapping 
summarizes the treatments received by a group of indi­
viduals in the network [8].
DTR estimation under interference networks is a rela­
tively recent area of research, and challenges include 
identifying interference structures and determining 
the ways in which characteristics or treatments of mul­
tiple individuals interact with one another. Although 
the exposure mapping is a low-dimensional summary of 
alter treatments to include as a tailoring variable in DTR 
methods, its true form is often unknown or difficult to 
represent due to the complex and often dynamic nature 
of social and spatial networks. Specification of the expo­
sure mapping to capture interference in a dataset, as well 
as the choice to include alter covariates in the ego’s out­
come model, may also affect the weighting criterion in 
regression-based DTR methods that is needed to ensure 
that estimators for treatment parameters are unbiased. 
Jiang et al. [7] first established balancing weight condi­
tions for dynamic weighted ordinary least squares regres­
sion (dWOLS) that guarantee consistency of estimators 
and maintain the double robustness property of standard 
dWOLS under interference networks. Mussavi Rizi et al. 
[13] proposed a joint optimization strategy for dWOLS 
to simultaneously maximize the outcome of two indi­
viduals in a dyadic network, i.e. a pair of individuals in a 
household. These analyses were applied to data from the 
Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health (PATH) 
study [14], where it was assumed that an ego’s cigarette 
dependence could be affected by an alter’s use of e-cig­
arettes in the same household [7]. In these analyses, 
every ego was assigned at least one alter within the same 
household under a partial inference or clustered interfer­
ence assumption, which restricts interference to occur 
only within some, but not between, groups of individuals 
to avoid the problem being computationally intractable 
[15–18].
While it is often left to the discretion of the researcher 
as to whether interference should be accounted for in an 
analysis, an interesting question arises when interference 
may be occurring among some, but not all, individuals in 
a network, rather than assuming complete violation of 
the SUTVA. For instance, if only inline-eq-IEq5 of households in 
the PATH dataset had individuals whose outcomes were 
affected by household members’ treatments, we may 
say that “interference is occurring among inline-eq-IEq6 of indi­
viduals selected for analysis”, and in cases such as this, a 
researcher using a “standard” dWOLS analysis that does 
not account for interference may still correctly estimate

the optimal treatment rule most of the time. However, if 
interference were taking place in the majority of house­
holds, say inline-eq-IEq7, then failing to account for interference in 
a DTR analysis may result in higher bias for the param­
eter estimates used to construct the optimal treatment 
rule. While in practice it is difficult to determine whether 
interference may be present in a dataset, context-specific 
factors can be used to determine whether it is suitable to 
account for interference in a personalized treatment rule. 
For example, previous research has already shown that 
phenomena related to substance use disorder (SUD) and 
addiction, such as drug use, smoking, and alcohol use, 
are associated with network structure, with behaviours 
spreading through close and distant social ties [19–23], 
and social relationships among people with SUD can 
be influenced by factors such as treatment participa­
tion [24–26]. There is also evidence that personalized 
interventions for treatment can be leveraged to enhance 
existing social network compositions, such as through 
support groups and one-on-one peer connections [27, 28].
Determining the extent to which interference is present 
in a dataset may also be important if the effect of a pre­
dictor in the optimal treatment rule depends on an alter’s 
treatment status. For example, if a caregiver is decid­
ing whether their patient (ego) would benefit from vir­
tual delivery of a peer support program to quit smoking 
or an in-person delivery, the optimal treatment rule for 
that individual may depend on several factors, including 
the person’s age and average number of days gone with­
out smoking, as well as their number of friends who have 
chosen an in-person delivery. However, the ego’s average 
number of days gone without smoking may be higher if 
they have more alters who have chosen the in-person 
delivery, but lower if they have more alters who have cho­
sen the virtual delivery. Whether or not the alters have 
chosen one versus another level of treatment will, in turn, 
depend on the overall probability of treatment assign­
ment in a given network setting, assuming that all indi­
viduals receive treatment independently of one another. 
In this paper, our aim is to illustrate how the extent and 
structure of interference occurring among individuals 
can affect the bias of estimated optimal treatment rules 
in DTR implementations with exposure mappings that 
either fail to account for interference, partially account 
for interference, or correctly specify the structure of 
interference present.

Varying the ratio of within and between group alters
Our results from the simulation study outlined in Simu­
lation study: varying the ratio of within- to between-
group alters section are summarized in Table 3. As the

when the alter’s component of the treatment free model 
is mis-specified, the MOTR treatment rates tend to be

researcher, a dWOLS implementation with mis-specified

mental health, such as having strong ties who are well-
educated and aware of their CF status [28]. When decid­
ing between online versus an in-person intervention for 
mental health care in this population, a health provider 
may tailor their patient’s treatment regimen based on the 
individual’s course of CF, as well as whether at least one 
of their network ties (such as patients from the same or 
different CF clinic) have been known to respond posi­
tively to online versus in-person treatment. However, if 
an online intervention is in its early stages of dissemi­
nation, or if an individual lacks strong network ties, an 
in-person intervention may be recommended instead, 
elucidating how the extent of interference as well as the 
overall rate of treatment are factors that can be adapted 
into DTR estimation as seen in our simulations.
Our work has several key limitations that motivate 
further development in DTRs under interference net­
works. In our simulations, we demonstrated within- and 
between-group interference occurring under hierarchi­
cal networks with random ego-alter assignments, but in 
practice, the probability of interacting with an alter from 
the same or a different group could be modelled to deter­
mine whether such a pairing should be made. In the case 
of an ego having an alter assigned from an independent 
group, their outcomes are likely to be correlated when 
the alter’s treatment affects their own individual outcome 
as well as the ego’s outcome. However, we emphasize that 
in our simulation studies, we are not attempting to max­
imize the expected outcomes of both the ego and their 
corresponding alters simultaneously, and every ego is 
assigned a distinct set of alters so that there is no pos­
sibility of two egos’ outcomes being correlated from the 
treatment effect of a shared alter. In our two-stage simu­
lation, the proportion of ego-alter assignments at stage 1 
remained the same in stage 2, and the stage 2 outcome 
did not depend on history from stage 1 given the stage 2 
covariates. Future work may investigate scenarios where 
the proportion of ego-alter assignments varies across 
stages; for instance, at the first stage of treatment, only 
inline-eq-IEq167 of individuals for analysis may be assigned an alter, 
but by stage two, this may increase to inline-eq-IEq168. Alternatively, 
the number of alters whose treatment affects an ego’s 
outcome may increase or decrease over stages to reflect 
the dynamic nature of social relationships.
DTR development under network interference remains 
a complex area of research that warrants careful con­
sideration of an ego’s social network dynamics and the 
nature of the condition for which they are being treated. 
Our work contributes to an understanding of how DTRs 
perform under settings where the extent of interference 
is not fully understood or only partially accounted for, 
which in turn can motivate the design of surveys or ques­
tionnaires prior to the data collection stage of a study. 
Future work investigating more complex applications of 
DTRs to varying interference structures through social 
and spatial networks have important applications in areas 
such as SUD and addiction management, infectious dis­
ease modelling, and pandemic preparedness.
