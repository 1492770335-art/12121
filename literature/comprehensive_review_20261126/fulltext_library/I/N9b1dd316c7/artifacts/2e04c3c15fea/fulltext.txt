Background: Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is a common and high-mortality complication in the intensive care 
unit (ICU), and renal replacement therapy (RRT) is frequently used, yet its survival benefits remain unclear. This study aims to assess 
survival differences via restricted mean survival time (RMST) in patients with SA-AKI.
Methods: This retrospective cohort study included 577 patients diagnosed with SA-AKI from the ICU of Shenzhen People’s Hospital 
between January 2017 and July 2019. All patients were categorized into RRT group (n=343) and non-RRT group (n=234) according to 
whether they need to received RRT. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves. Univariate and multivariable 
logistic regression analyses were employed for the selection of covariates. RMST analysis was performed to quantify the survival 
benefits associated with RRT.
Results: Patients who need to received RRT had greater baseline illness severity and more frequent organ support (eg, higher SOFA 
scores and higher rates of shock and mechanical ventilation), suggesting potential confounding by indication. In unadjusted analyses, 
28d mortality was higher in the RRT group than in the non-RRT group (44% vs 20%). After adjustment for multidrug-resistant (MDR) 
infection and SOFA score (both P < 0.05), the RMST difference between the RRT and non-RRT groups was 3.25 days (95% CI: 0.67 
to 5.84; p = 0.01), which was statistically significant.
Conclusion: In this retrospective cohort study, after adjusting for SOFA score and MDR infection, the potential survival benefits of 
RRT in patients with SA-AKI were demonstrated. These findings suggest that poor outcomes in RRT recipients may be attributable to 
underlying risk factors rather than RRT itself. However, given the observational design, these results are hypothesis-generating and 
require prospective validation.
Keywords: sepsis, acute renal injury, renal replacement therapy, hemofiltration, restricted mean survival time, mortality rate

injury, and organ hypoperfusion.2,3 The kidneys are particularly vulnerable to these insults, resulting in sepsis-associated 
acute kidney injury (SA-AKI), which contributes to elevated mortality rates and poor prognoses.4,5 For the management 
of patients with SA-AKI, in addition to fundamental interventions such as antimicrobial therapy and fluid resuscitation, 
renal replacement therapy (RRT) is frequently employed for the purposes of volume management and the clearance of 
metabolic byproducts and toxins.6 RRT constitutes a significant supportive measure in the management of severe acute 
kidney injury within the intensive care unit (ICU).7
Nevertheless, within actual ICU populations, individuals undergoing RRT frequently demonstrate elevated crude 
mortality rates. A recent multicenter study revealed that the proportion of patients receiving RRT in ICUs worldwide 
ranged from 3.3% to 9.2%. Furthermore, the in-hospital mortality rate for critically ill patients undergoing RRT was 
nearly four times higher (57.1%) compared to those not receiving RRT (16.8%).8 This observation may imply the 
presence of adverse effects associated with RRT or restricted therapeutic advantages. However, patients receiving RRT 
are intrinsically more critically ill (such as being in shock, requiring vasopressors or mechanical ventilation, having 
higher Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, etc).9,10 The optimal role of RRT in SA-AKI remains 
controversial. Recent meta-analyses have yielded conflicting conclusions regarding its survival benefit. For instance, 
a 2024 meta-analysis of 12 studies involving 3,648 SA-AKI patients reported that early RRT was associated with a lower 
28-day mortality (RR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.59-0.88), although this benefit was limited to patients with SOFA ≤ 12 and did not 
extend to 90-day outcomes.11 Conversely, another meta-analysis of 9 high-quality randomized controlled trials compris­
ing 1,042 participants found that early RRT initiation does not improve patient survival or confer reductions in ICU or 
hospital length of stay (LOS).12 Furthermore, the comparative effectiveness of different RRT strategies, including 
modality choice and dose, remains uncertain.13,14 Consequently, a mere comparison of the raw outcomes between 
RRT and non-RRT groups is prone to confounding influences, including disease severity, characteristics of the infection, 
and the intensity of organ support, which may result in inconclusive findings regarding the actual direction and 
magnitude of the association with RRT.
Most existing studies have relied on traditional survival methods, such as the Kaplan–Meier curve and Cox 
proportional hazards model, frequently depend on the proportional hazards assumption, wherein the hazard ratio (HR) 
does not intuitively reflect an actual difference in survival time. In contrast, Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) 
analysis is independent of the proportional hazards assumption and directly quantifies the difference in mean survival 
time between two groups within a specified time window,15 making it more clinically interpretable. This study aims to 
evaluate the association between RRT administration and mortality/survival time outcomes in SA-AKI patients within 
a single-center ICU retrospective cohort, while also identifying key high-risk factors that may obscure or influence this 
association, thus providing a foundation for more tailored RRT selection and risk stratification.
Methods
Study Design, Setting, and Data Source
This study is a single-center retrospective cohort study, including patients diagnosed with sepsis who fulfilled the SA- 
AKI criteria and were admitted to the Department of Intensive Care Medicine at Shenzhen People’s Hospital between 
January 2017 and July 2019. Data were extracted from electronic medical records and the ICU information system. This 
study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board of the original research with a waiver of informed consent, 
and performed in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later 
amendments.
Participants: Eligibility Criteria and Clinical Definitions
Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) who met Sepsis-3 criteria and subsequently developed AKI defined by KDIGO 
criteria during ICU stay. SA-AKI was defined as the occurrence of AKI within the context of sepsis, following the 
exclusion of alternative etiologies for AKI, such as exposure to nephrotoxic agents, significant hemorrhage, or urinary 
tract obstructions.

Clinical Intervention and Exposure
The overall management of septic patients follows the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines. Before administering 
antibiotics, patients entering the ICU undergo blood routine tests, blood microbial cultures, and assessments of organ 
function indicators. Vasoactive drugs were administered to shock patients, with norepinephrine as the first choice. 
Mechanical ventilation (MV) was applied to patients with respiratory failure (RF). The decision to initiate RRT in 
patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) or chronic kidney disease (CKD) was based on KDIGO-guided indications (eg, 
refractory volume overload, progressive azotemia, metabolic acidosis pH < 7.2, hyperkalemia > 6.5 mmol/L, or oliguria/ 
anuria despite adequate resuscitation), integrated with the attending physician’s clinical judgment regarding the patient’s 
overall trajectory (eg, worsening lactate, increasing vasopressor requirements). This real-world, non-standardized 
approach reflects routine clinical practice and was not protocolized to preserve the pragmatic nature of the cohort. The 
RRT group was subjected to continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH) utilizing an ST 150 filter. Initially, all 
patients in this cohort received CVVH continuously for 24 hours per day during the first three days. Subsequently, the 
duration was adjusted to 16 hours per day until RRT was no longer necessary or the patients were transferred out of the 
ICU. The prescribed ultrafiltration rate of CVVH was 30 mL/kg/h with a blood flow rate of 150 mL/min.
Observational Index
Gender, age, organ failure, and organ support measures were documented. Organ function indicators, inflammatory 
markers (such as white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, procalcitonin, C-reactive protein, etc), and 
clinical outcomes (death/survival) were recorded. The LOS in the ICU and duration of MV (measured in hours) were also 
documented.
Outcomes and Follow-up
The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. Time-to-event was quantified from the point of ICU admission 
until death occurred within 28 days. Patients who remained alive at day 28, regardless of whether they were still in the 
ICU or had been discharged were treated as censored data. In the RMST framework, the restricted mean survival time is 
defined as the area under the survival curve up to a prespecified truncation time τ. For this study, τ was set at 28 days to 
capture clinically meaningful short-term survival differences during the acute phase of critical illness, consistent with 
widely used endpoints in sepsis trials. Under this definition, patients who died before day 28 contributed their actual 
observed survival time (< 28 days), whereas those who survived beyond day 28 were assigned a survival time of 28 days 
and treated as censored observations in the RMST analysis. Based on these estimates, the RMST difference (RMST-D) 
and RMST ratio (RMST-R) between the two groups were calculated.
Covariates
The prespecified covariates included demographic characteristics (sex, age), disease severity and organ support (SOFA 
score, shock, vasopressors, mechanical ventilation), infection-related characteristics, and early laboratory indicators at 
ICU admission presented in Table 1. These laboratory indicators comprised complete blood count parameters (red blood 
cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, red cell distribution width-coefficient of variation, white blood cell count, lympho­
cyte count, platelet count, mean platelet volume(MPV), platelet distribution width(PDW)), coagulation and biochemistry 
parameters (antithrombin III(AT III), activated partial thromboplastin time(APTT), blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, 
blood urea nitrogen to serum creatinine ratio, total bilirubin), lipid profile (total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, 
low-density lipoprotein, triglycerides), inflammatory markers (procalcitonin, C-reactive protein), and other parameters 
(glycosylated hemoglobin, albumin).

The main analysis used RMST to evaluate the difference in survival time between RRT and non-RRT groups, 
adjusting for predefined covariates. For covariate-adjusted RMST analysis, estimation was performed using the method 
proposed by Tian et al,16 which extends the standard RMST framework to adjust for baseline covariates within a Cox- 
type modeling framework. This approach regresses the restricted mean survival time on covariates through a pseudo- 
observation technique, yielding adjusted RMST estimates and their between-group differences. Candidate covariates for 
inclusion in the adjusted model were selected based on clinical plausibility and univariate screening with a threshold of 
p < 0.05. Variables reaching this threshold were then entered into a multivariable Cox regression model with a backward 
elimination procedure (retention criterion: p < 0.05) to identify independent predictors of 28-day mortality. Collinearity 
among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and no significant multicollinearity was detected 
(all VIF < 2). The final adjusted RMST model included variables that were independently associated with mortality in the 
multivariate analysis, all of which are presented in the baseline characteristics table (Table 1). Kaplan-Meier curves were 
also plotted to describe unadjusted survival differences and Log rank tests were conducted.
Results
Study Population and Baseline Characteristics
In total, 577 patients meeting the diagnostic criteria for SA-AKI were included in this retrospective real-world study, with 
an average age of 61.89±19.33 years. Among them, there were 365 male and 212 female patients. The SOFA score was 
11.70±4.99. Patients were divided into the RRT group (n=343) and the non-RRT group (n=234) based on whether they 
need to received RRT during ICU stay.
Infection sites in both groups included the lungs, abdomen, biliary tract, urinary tract, and bloodstream. The RRT 
group had a significantly higher incidence of multiple drug resistant (MDR) infections (p<0.001), whereas no statistically 
significant differences were observed in the rates of other infection types between the two groups (all p>0.05). Baseline 
characteristics for both groups are detailed in Table 1. Furthermore, patients in the RRT group exhibited higher rates of 
MV, and shock, along with elevated SOFA score, compared to the non-RRT group, indicating a more severe clinical 
status upon ICU admission. Clinical outcomes for the two groups are presented in Table 2.
Unadjusted Associations with 28d Mortality
In unadjusted analyses, patients who received RRT had a significantly higher 28-day mortality rate than those who did 
not (44% vs 20%; p < 0.001). The RMST analysis revealed an RMST of 24.80 days (95% CI: 23.24, 26.36) in the non- 
RRT group compared to 21.03 days (95% CI: 19.39, 22.67) in the RRT group. The restricted mean time lost (RMTL) was 
3.20 days (95% CI: 1.65, 4.76) in the non-RRT group, and 6.97 days (95% CI: 5.33, 8.61) in the RRT group. Kaplan- 
Meier survival curves are shown in Figure 1A.

Univariate analysis revealed that receipt of RRT was significantly associated with increased 28d mortality [OR=3.092, 
95% CI: 2.105–4.542), P<0.001]. In addition to RRT, septicemia, higher SOFA score, elevated PDW and MPV, and 
prolonged APTT were also independently associated with greater mortality risk. Conversely, higher AT III levels was 
associated with reduced mortality risk (see Table 3).
Covariate-Adjusted Survival and RMST
Multivariate analysis revealed that the mortality rate in the RRT group was independently associated with MDR infection 
and higher SOFA scores (both p<0.05, Table 4). In the covariate-adjusted analysis, we adjusted for these key high-risk 
factors within the RMST framework to obtain adjusted survival curves and adjusted RMST estimates. The adjusted 
Kaplan-Meier curve shows a reversal in survival outcomes between the two groups, with the RRT group showing 
a significant survival advantage (Log rank test p=0.0012).
Accordingly, the adjusted RMST was 18.7 days in the non-RRT group versus 22.2 days in the RRT group (p=0.0136). 
The Kaplan-Meier survival curves are shown in Figure 1B. The RMST-D [(arm=1)–(arm=0)], RMST ratio [(arm=1)/ 
(arm=0)], and RMTL ratio [(arm=1)/(arm=0)] with and without covariate adjustment are listed in Table 5.
Exploratory RMST-Based Models
Furthermore, exploratory model analyses were conducted under different RMST and RMTL frameworks to identify 
factors associated with reduced RMST or increased RMTL. The results indicate that in the RMST-D model, MDR 
infections and SOFA score were significantly associated with a reduced RMST within 28 days (both p < 0.05), suggesting 
that these factors attenuated the potential survival advantage of RRT. In the RMST ratio model, MDR infections and 
SOFA score similarly showed a statistically significant association with a reduction in the RMST ratio (p = 0.025, < 
0.001, respectively), further confirming their detrimental impact on short-term survival. In the RMTL ratio model, SOFA 
score remained significant predictors of increased time lost within the 28-day window (p = 0.049 and < 0.001, 
respectively), whereas MDR infection showed a consistent direction of association but did not reach statistical sig­
nificance (p = 0.060) (see Table 6).
Discussion
SA-AKI is a frequent organ failure observed in the ICU, however, the potential effectiveness of RRT in improving the 
prognosis of SA-AKI patients remains debatable RRT in sepsis is instrumental in eliminating excess fluids and mediators, 
thereby endowing it with exceptional fluid balance capabilities.17 Nevertheless, recent clinical studies have presented 
inconclusive evidence regarding the efficacy of RRT in managing SA-AKI.8 There is no demonstrated advantage in 
initiating RRT early for patients with AKI or SA-AKI.18,19 In reality, it is challenging to treat SA-AKI patients at an early 
stage in clinical practice, as these patients are often outside the ICU, in emergency or other specialized departments. 
Overall, the efficacy of RRT does not align with clinical expectations. Consequently, we scrutinize real-world data from 
SA-AKI patients to elucidate the reasons behind the suboptimal effectiveness of RRT. Simultaneously, we expand upon 
the application of restricted mean survival time (RMST) analysis in time-to-event outcomes.

The RMST approach offers several advantages over conventional survival methods in this context. Unlike the hazard 
ratio, which summarizes relative risk under the proportional hazards assumption and may be challenging to interpret 
when this assumption is violated, RMST directly quantifies the absolute difference in average survival time between two 
groups within a clinically meaningful time horizon.15,20,21 In our study, the adjusted RMST difference of 3.5 days within 
28 days provides a tangible estimate of the survival gain associated with RRT. This absolute metric is more intuitively 
interpretable for clinicians and patients than a relative risk reduction. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply 
RMST analysis specifically to quantify the survival benefit of RRT in SA-AKI, thereby complementing existing evidence 
derived from traditional Cox regression or simple group comparisons.
Our findings suggest that factors such as multidrug resistance (MDR) infections and SOFA scores are associated with 
worse outcomes in RRT recipients, and that these factors may confound the apparent relationship between RRT and 
mortality. Previous studies have also identified these factors as significant contributors to the chronicity of sepsis and 
elevated mortality rates.22,23 After adjusting for the identified high-risk factors, the survival relationship between the two 
groups underwent a notable reversal.24,25 In the unadjusted analysis, RRT recipients had a higher 28-day mortality rate 
and a shorter unadjusted RMST, reflecting the confounding effect of greater baseline illness severity in the RRT group. 
However, after covariate adjustment, the RRT group demonstrated a significantly longer adjusted RMST. RMST ratio>1 
indicates that the RRT group has a longer survival time compared to the control group. In other words, the survival of the 
RRT group was better than that of the control group, which may indicate a positive therapeutic effect. At the same time, 
the RMTL ratio less than 1 indicates that the RRT group has less time loss compared to the control group during 28 days. 
This means that during this period, the RRT group had a lower incidence of death, demonstrating better survival benefits.

Recent large-scale studies have advanced our understanding of SA-AKI, particularly its risk factors and prognostic 
heterogeneity. In a multicenter cohort of 84,528 ICU admissions, White et al reported that SA-AKI occurred in one-sixth 
of patients, typically diagnosed within 24 hours of ICU admission, with those diagnosed by low urine output alone 
having significantly lower mortality (12% vs 15%–35%) and RRT requirements (2.8% vs 18%–50%) than those 
diagnosed by creatinine criteria.26 Extending this epidemiological framework, Gomez et al, in a cohort of 190,550 
critically ill patients, identified high SOFA score, sepsis, shock, and high fluid balance as the most important risk factors 
for persistent severe AKI. This condition was associated with a 1.5-fold increased 90-day mortality and a markedly lower 
probability of renal recovery (OR 0.14).27 Together, these studies underscore that illness severity, as reflected by SOFA 
score and organ dysfunction burden, is the primary driver of progression along the AKI continuum. This notion aligns 
with our finding that SOFA score was a key confounder in the association between RRT and survival.
Regarding the efficacy of specific RRT strategies, Wang et al, in a meta-analysis of 14 studies including 695 sepsis 
patients, reported that the oXiris adsorptive filter was associated with a lower 28-day mortality (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.36– 
0.77), although the evidence certainty was rated as low or very low.11 Park et al, in a randomized controlled trial of 73 
SA-AKI patients, found that BIA-guided volume control did not improve survival, but a post-hoc analysis revealed that 
an achieved volume accumulation rate >50% was independently associated with a markedly increased 90-day mortality 
(HR 7.18, 95% CI 1.58–32.51).28 These findings highlight that the actual physiological response to therapy, rather than 
the assigned strategy itself, may play a more critical role in driving patient outcomes. Our study complements these 
observations by demonstrating that, after adjusting for severity markers including SOFA score and MDR infection, RRT 
was associated with a significant 28-day survival benefit. This benefit was entirely obscured in unadjusted analyses due to 
confounding by indication. Together, these data suggest that accurate risk adjustment and careful patient selection are 
essential to unmask the true effect of RRT in SA-AKI. Future research should focus on identifying which subgroups, 
such as those with high inflammatory burden or modifiable risk factors like MDR infection, derive the greatest benefit 
from RRT.
In this study, all patients utilized ST 150 filters (Prismaflex) with a membrane surface area of 1.5 m2, primarily 
designed for blood filtration and volume management. However, the optimal filtration strategy in SA-AKI remains 
uncertain. The IVOIRE trial, the largest randomized trial comparing high-volume (70 mL/kg/h) with standard-volume 
(35 mL/kg/h) hemofiltration in septic shock with AKI, found no 28-day mortality benefit with high-volume hemofiltra­
tion. A subsequent pharmacokinetic substudy of the IVOIRE cohort further demonstrated that high-volume hemofiltra­
tion significantly shortened the elimination half-life of most antibiotics and increased their total clearance, leading the 
authors to warn that current dosing recommendations may expose patients to underdosing and increase the risk of 
treatment failure and resistance.29 This observation is particularly relevant to our finding that MDR infection was 
significantly more prevalent in the RRT group (25.1% vs 9.8%) and independently associated with mortality. This 
suggests that RRT-associated antibiotic clearance may contribute to worse outcomes in patients with high inflammatory 
burden, and that optimizing antimicrobial dosing during RRT may be as important as the filtration strategy itself. Beyond 
volume optimization, adsorptive filters (eg, CytoSorb, oXiris, HA series) have shown promise in cytokine removal in 
small studies,30–34 but their impact on mortality remains unproven, and none have been incorporated into sepsis 
guidelines. These considerations highlight that RRT in SA-AKI should be viewed not merely as a renal support modality, 
but as an intervention with complex interactions with antimicrobial therapy and infection control. These areas warrant 
further investigation. These considerations suggest that in SA-AKI patients with documented MDR infections, the 
potential attenuation of RRT benefit should be weighed against the need for renal support. Optimizing source control 
and targeted antimicrobial therapy may be particularly critical in this subgroup, and RRT-associated antibiotic clearance 
should be carefully monitored.
Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the decision to initiate RRT was not strictly 
standardized, and the exact timing of RRT initiation relative to AKI diagnosis or ICU admission was not recorded. 
Although we have now clarified in the Methods section that RRT decisions followed KDIGO-guided indications 
integrated with clinical judgment, this real-world, non-protocolized approach inevitably introduces confounding by

Conclusion
This study demonstrates the potential survival benefits of RRT in patients with SA-AKI. However, these benefits may be 
obscured by high-risk factors that negatively influence clinical outcomes, including MDR infections and SOFA score. 
These modifiable risk factors may inform risk stratification and guide targeted interventions in the early management of 
SA-AKI. However, owing to the observational design, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather 
than confirmatory. Further research is needed to optimize the individualized application of RRT and to develop targeted 
interventions that effectively mitigate these risk factors, thereby improving its clinical efficacy.
